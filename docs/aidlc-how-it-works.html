01The flow

The whole lifecycle, end to end

The source methodology has ten phases in sequence. All ten survive here. We grouped them into three enforced boundaries. A phase was a heading that someone typed. A gate is a merge that a machine checks and a human approves.

Starts here A customer need, in their own words saved word for word, so every requirement has a source

Gate 1 · Inception

Discovery

Are we building the right thing?

Who drafts
/ba/ux/architect advises/manager plans
What comes out
Three pull requests, in order. First, a requirements document with numbered, testable requirements. Second, one screen spec per screen with every state numbered, plus design tokens. Third, user stories with numbered acceptance criteria. In parallel, an advisory DB and app architecture and an advisory delivery plan.
The distinctive step
A grill pass. The persona asks about each ambiguity, one question at a time, before it drafts anything. Each question that stays open becomes an open question with a named owner. A guess never becomes a requirement.

Human approves Three reviews, in order. The product owner merges the requirements. The designer approves the screen structure, and the product team approves the styling. Then the product owner merges the stories. Each merge commit is the scope baseline for that step.

Gate 2 · Construction

Delivery

Does this story work, with proof?

Who drafts
/dev/qa/architect
What comes out
First the developer persona tiers the task as Simple, Medium, or Complex, by the riskiest surface it touches (a contract, stored data, trust, a dependency, or an operational surface). Medium and Complex work gets a spec package for the story. It holds technical requirements traced to the acceptance criteria, an implementation plan, an impact analysis, decisions, and traceability. Simple work gets one row in a change log. Then one pull request holds everything: the code, tests named after the criteria they prove, a decision record if a real trade-off existed, and the updated traceability links.
The distinctive step
A human reviews the plan before the code exists. The developer writes the implementation plan and the impact analysis first. A human approves them in chat. This is the cheapest moment to catch a wrong assumption. Then tests come first, from the requirement. QA derives tests from the story before it reads the code. So a passing test has failed at least once.

Human approves, twice D1: the written implementation plan, in chat. The persona stamps the approver, the date, and the SHA of the plan into the plan file, and the developer commits the stamp. The SHA is verifiable, so a later edit is detectable. The name comes from git config, so D1 is attribution, not authentication. A team that needs more requests a GitHub review on the same branch. D2: the story PR. A human reviews it and merges it after every required check is green. Merged means delivered. Nothing between D1 and D2 needs a human.

Gate 3 · Operations

Release

Is production safe?

Who drafts
/devops/manager reports
What comes out
The pipeline itself, the releases, a current rollback procedure, and monitoring tied to the performance budgets from the requirements.
The distinctive step
Promotion goes through the pipeline only. A step that a person must do by hand is a pipeline bug, filed as an issue. Nobody switches off a red quality step to unblock a release.

Human approves A human approves each promotion in the pipeline: dev, then QA, then production. Each approval is authenticated, per environment.

Inside Gate 1: scope locks from left to right

Discovery runs as three pull requests in order, with stories last (ADR-003). So nobody writes a story against scope that still moves. Design runs in two passes (ADR-004). The structure pass produces the screen spec, and the designer approves it. The styling pass produces tokens and rendered previews, and the product team approves it. Hi-fi frames stay in the design tool. The charter enforces this order. CI does not. The personas refuse to run ahead, and the human review holds the line. We chose this over a new gate check.

1

Requirements freeze

/ba grills, then drafts the requirements document. The product owner merges it. After that, the requirements cannot move.

2

Design approved

/ux drafts each screen from the requirement it serves. The designer approves the structure. The product team approves the styling. If a styling change forces a structural change, the structure pass reopens.

3

Stories sliced

Only then does /ba slice the stories. Each story cites frozen requirements and approved screens. This merge is the locked scope that Gate 2 builds against.

∥

Advisory, in parallel

After the requirements freeze, /architect drafts the DB and app architecture in its own PR. It blocks nothing. /manager proposes a delivery plan with estimates and two-week sprints. The human locks the plan in Jira. Neither one gates anything.

Ends here Production and then the loop starts again:

The loop. An incident, a defect, or a change of mind gets no bureaucracy of its own. It becomes a GitHub issue, labeled bug or change-request. A change request reopens Gate 1 for the affected artifacts. A bug lands as a fix PR that must add a regression test. Postmortem lessons become edges in the traceability graph. Nothing is learned off the record.

02The people

Seven personas, seven real counterparts

One persona per human who can approve its work. A persona with nobody to report to produces output that nobody owns. You type your command. The persona interviews you and prepares the paperwork. You never write a prompt.

/aidlc

Anyone · start here

The front door. It works out who you are and what you need, then becomes the right persona.

You"I'm new. What is this project?"
GetA briefing. Then it continues as your persona. No second command.

/ba

Business analyst · product owner

Turns a need into requirements and stories. Owns what the product must do.

You"Planners need to compare shipping routes by cost and time."
GetA requirements PR first. Stories come later, after design is approved. You get one link to review each time.

/ux

Designer

Turns approved requirements into screen specs, structure first and then styling, and into the design system. Owns what the user sees and does.

You"Design the results screen."
GetEvery state numbered, plus a token file that you import into Figma or Penpot.

/architect

Solution architect · tech lead

Drafts the DB and app architecture after the requirements freeze. Advises where a trade-off is real. Reviews every story PR. Advisory only: it cannot edit the repository. A reviewed PR lands what it drafts.

You"Review this pull request."
GetRated findings and a suggested verdict. Your review is the authority.

/dev

Developer

Implements one approved story as one PR: code, tests, and real evidence pasted in. It never runs git commit or git push. It leaves the changes in the working tree with a suggested commit message, and the developer commits.

You"Build the next ready story."
GetA tier, a written plan to approve, then a failing test first and the code that makes it green.

/qa

QA engineer

Derives tests from the requirement before it reads the code. Files defects that anyone can reproduce. For browser-level work it writes a step-by-step plan, and you approve the plan before any test exists. Then it generates Playwright tests against the running app.

You"This screen shows the wrong total."
GetA defect issue with steps and real output. The fix must add a regression test.

/devops

DevOps engineer

Owns the path from merge to production: pipelines, promotions, rollback.

You"Why is the build red?"
GetThe failing step, the cause, and a fix in a normal PR.

/manager

Delivery manager

Reports, routes, and proposes the delivery plan. You lock the estimates and sprints in Jira. It reads status from GitHub only. It holds no gate authority and cannot edit the repository.

You"Where are we? What's stuck?"
GetStatus derived from pull requests, issues, and checks. Never from a document.

Two personas are read-only by tooling

The framework always said that the architect's review is advisory and that the manager holds no gate authority. Those were words. Now both personas declare the write tools as disallowed. They cannot change a file. If anyone removes that restriction, the validator fails the build. This holds on Claude Code and opencode. Cursor and Copilot cannot restrict the tools of an agent, so there the two personas carry a read-only notice and the charter rule stands on its own.

Each persona exists three times

A charter (the contract), a command that you type, and an agent that you delegate to for parallel work. The validator proves that all three agree. So a persona cannot drift from its contract without a red build.

The one limit that no tool enforces

No persona can merge, approve, or close anything. A shell is a shell, so this is a charter rule that ranks above every other instruction. The framework says so plainly. It does not imply that CI catches it.

03Using it

One relay, hand-off by hand-off

One human owns each stage and types one command. The persona interviews you and prepares a pull request. Your merge passes the baton. The persona always ends by telling you who runs next and what they type.

Installing it, once per repository

1

Scaffold

One person runs /aidlc-init in Claude Code, or npx github:ss-trigent/aidlc from any shell. It copies the framework, pins the personas into the repo for all four editors, seeds the traceability manifest, and lands as a reviewed PR.

2

Tailor

An interview rewrites ai/standards/ and writes ai/project-context.md for your stack. These files, the Jira templates, the manifest, and your CI are yours. Everything else is hash-locked (check 14).

3

Wire CI

Add node tools/aidlc-check.mjs to CI and mark it required in branch protection. This step turns guidance into governance.

4

Update later

npx github:ss-trigent/aidlc --update refreshes every framework-owned file and skips what you own. An empty git diff means you are current. A rule you want changed goes upstream as a change-request issue.

Teammates clone the repo. Nothing to install per person.

/ba
Gate 1 · requirements The BA or product owner types /ba and describes the customer need in their own words. Pasted meeting notes work too. The persona grills every ambiguity first, drafts the requirements document, and opens the PR when you ask. Your merge freezes the requirements and hands off to the designer.
/ux
Gate 1 · design, structure The designer types /ux. The persona writes one screen spec per requirement that needs UI. Every state gets a number, ST-##. Open conflicts stay listed until someone resolves them. The designer approves the structure.
/ux
Gate 1 · design, styling Same persona, second pass. It produces design tokens for both themes and previews that render every state with styling. The designer takes the exported tokens into Figma or Penpot for hi-fi frames. The frames stay in the tool. The product team approves the styling. Cycle: if a styling change forces a structural change, the structure pass reopens.
/ba
Gate 1 · stories The BA returns. Requirements and screens are frozen, so only now does the persona slice stories. Each story cites its requirements, its screen, and numbered acceptance criteria. The merge by the product owner locks the scope. Gate 1 is done.
∥ advisory
In parallel, after the requirements freeze /architect drafts the DB and app architecture in its own PR. It blocks nothing. /manager proposes the delivery plan with estimates and two-week sprints. You lock the plan in Jira. Neither one gates the relay.
/dev
Gate 2 · one story, one PR The developer types /dev and names a ready story, or asks for the next one. The persona tiers the task, then writes the spec package on a feat/US-### branch before any code. A human approves the plan inside the package, and that approval unblocks the work. After that, the persona writes a failing test per criterion first, then the code that makes it green. The developer commits. The persona pastes the evidence into the PR.
/qa
Gate 2 · same PR The QA engineer types /qa. The persona derives tests from the story before it reads the diff: a positive test per criterion, then negative tests, then boundary tests. Browser-level criteria are an opt-in layer (--profile e2e, ADR-006): the persona writes a plan, you approve it, and only then does it generate Playwright tests against the running app. Some behavior can never be automated, for example a real email that arrives, a device, or a visual judgment. That behavior becomes a written manual case in the spec package. Most stories have none. Wrong behavior found later becomes a bug issue. Its fix PR must add a regression test.
/architect
Gate 2 · review, then merge Anyone asks /architect to review the PR. The result is rated findings and a suggested verdict, and it is advisory. A human merges when every check is green. Merged means delivered. Cycle: run /dev, then /qa, then review, for every story in the locked scope.
/devops
Gate 3 · release The DevOps engineer types /devops for pipeline work. Promotion runs dev, then QA, then production. You approve each step in the pipeline, never outside it.

The cycles. A defect becomes a bug issue, then a fix/<issue#>-<slug> PR with a regression test. A change of mind about approved scope becomes a change-request issue. Then the relay reruns from the affected Gate 1 step (requirements, design, or stories), with the same PRs and the same reviews. Production lessons land as lessons edges in the traceability graph. /manager tells you where the baton is at any moment. /aidlc is the front door when you do not know which command is yours.

Persona or agent: two ways to run the same role

You are doing your own stage

Type the command

  • /ba, /ux, /dev, /qa, /devops, /architect, /manager Conversational. It asks you one question at a time, in plain language, and prepares the paperwork. You never touch git or file paths.
  • /aidlc When you are not sure. It works out who you are and becomes the right persona.
  • Any editor The same commands exist in Cursor, opencode, and Copilot Chat. They are generated from the same charters, and CI fails if they drift (ADR-005).

Work runs alongside you

Delegate the agent

  • aidlc-ba · aidlc-ux · aidlc-dev · aidlc-qa · aidlc-devops · aidlc-architect · aidlc-manager The same persona as a subagent that you delegate to, for parallel or background work. For example: two stories under two aidlc-dev agents, a coverage check handed to aidlc-qa mid-session, or an independent PR review from aidlc-architect.
  • One contract, proven The charter in ai/roles/ binds both surfaces. CI fails if they drift apart. The advisory agents (aidlc-architect, aidlc-manager) cannot write files at all.

Either way, no persona can merge, approve, or close. That is always you, in GitHub.

The rhythm at every hand-off

1

You talk

Type your command and describe what you have in your own words. Paste meeting notes if that is easier. The persona asks one question at a time, in plain language, and offers a sensible default.

2

You get grilled

The persona pushes on vague answers before it writes anything. It asks "what happens when...?" for every edge. Each question that stays open is recorded with your name on it. Nothing is invented in silence.

3

You get a walkthrough

Not a file dump. Each item in one sentence, what the persona decided for you, and what is still your call. A repository is not a readable format for an approver. The conversation is.

4

You click Approve

One GitHub link. Two clicks. This records who approved exactly what, bound to an exact commit. Then the persona tells you who is next and what they type.

Approval is a GitHub review. Never text in a document. Never a message in a chat.

This is the decision that holds up the whole framework. Anyone can type a document header that says "Approved by: Priya", and anyone can edit it later. A pull-request review binds a verified identity to a specific commit, permanently. The first implementation of this methodology used document headers. An independent review called it what it was: compliance theater.

04Worked example

One real story, all the way through

These are not illustrations. They are the artifacts of the reference project that the framework was built in, ss-trigent/logistics-shortest-route-poc, with their real identifiers. This framework repo holds no product artifacts, and its manifest is empty by design. Solid rungs were built and machine-checked there. Dashed rungs show a project that had not started to build when the framework was extracted on 3 August 2026.

REQ-003
Gate 1 · the requirement "Each returned route lists its legs: mode, from and to, scheduled departure and arrival, cost, duration, total transfers." Testable and sourced. It traces to the project charter, not to someone's memory.
US-003
Gate 1 · the story "View ranked route results." It cites REQ-003 and the usability requirement NFR-003. The link is bidirectional and validated. A requirement cannot point at a story that does not point back.

Why the chain matters

Ask "why does this line of code exist?" The answer is a path back to a sentence that a customer said. Ask "what breaks if we drop this requirement?" The graph lists the stories, screens, states, components, and tests that hang off it, before anyone edits anything.

05Enforcement

One command decides if any of this is real

The operating principle is blunt. A rule that CI or a GitHub setting does not check is guidance, not governance. Sixteen checks run on every pull request as one required status.

1

Every identifier is unique across the whole repository. No two documents claim the same number.

2

Every link resolves in both directions. Every requirement that a story cites is a node in the graph. Nothing hangs off a one-way link.

3

The criteria list matches the story file exactly. A criterion cannot vanish between the document and the graph.

4

A passing, active test names every criterion. A story in delivery with no tests fails. A story that the traceability graph does not know fails too. Otherwise it would slip past this rule. Citations in comments or in skipped tests are not proof.

5

Test files that cite a story are listed against it. A stale graph cannot under-report coverage.

6

Every product project can run tests. Untestable code is a build failure, not an oversight.

7

The published traceability matrix matches what the graph generates. No hand-edited view can flatter reality.

8

Decision and lesson links point at real artifacts. No dangling references.

9

The distributable copy of the framework matches its source. Other teams cannot install a drifted version.

10

The three surfaces of every persona agree, and the two advisory personas still cannot write. Authority is built into the tooling.

11

Design is complete. A story with UI cites a screen. A screen traces to a story or to the requirement it serves. A component renders every numbered state. The exported token file matches the stylesheet. Eight sub-rules.

12

Ticket templates are safe. No template declares a field that forwards approval or duplicates what the tracker owns. No unknown placeholders. No stale markup. See section 07.

13

The Cursor, opencode, and Copilot persona surfaces match their sources. They are generated files, never hand-edited. ADR-005.

14

Nobody can edit the framework itself in silence. Every framework-owned file matches a shipped hash lock. An adopting team edits only its own standards, ticket templates, and project context. A different rule goes upstream as a change request.

15

Browser-test evidence from another repository is checked, or absent. A QA team that keeps its end-to-end tests in its own repo publishes an evidence file. The validator checks that every criterion the file claims exists, and that every pass carries the run it came from. No file means no requirement. The rule does not touch a team whose tests live in this repo. The end-to-end layer · ADR-006.

16

A development spec package, if one exists, tells the truth. Every technical requirement in the spec has a row in the traceability table. Every file that the table points at exists. Every story and criterion that it names is real. The package is listed in the spec index. The approval block in the plan records who approved it, when, and which version. So a plan edited after approval fails unless the change is logged. Where the Jira lane is installed, a package whose story has no ticket key is a warning, never an error. A story with no package fails nothing, because the validator cannot know how big the task was. The development spec packages · ADR-007.

What it deliberately cannot prove

It cannot prove that the assertions in a test are meaningful, or that a screen state is handled well. An empty test body with the right name satisfies the tooling. The reviewer judges quality. That is why every gate still ends at a human.

And the one gap that undermines all of it Open

Branch protection is not available on the plan of this repository. Every check above shows red or green on each pull request, but nothing blocks a merge. This is an administrative setting, not code. It is the highest-value open item in the whole system. An adopting team on GitHub Pro or a public repo does not have this gap.

The identifier scheme, in full

REQ-###A functional requirement
NFR-###Speed, usability, reliability
RISK-###A recorded risk
US-###A user story
AC-##An acceptance criterion, scoped to its story
SCR-###A screen specification
ST-##A screen state, scoped to its screen
ADR-###A decision with a real trade-off

Defects and change requests are GitHub issue numbers, not documents. Test cases are test names, not records. The personas explain each identifier the first time you see it. They never ask you to remember one.

06Design

Design is a handoff, not a tool we own

This is the change that most affects how a designer joins. The repository sits upstream of the tool that the designer already uses. The repository holds what a gate can check. The tool holds what a gate cannot check.

Canonical · CI-enforced · in the pull request

The repository

  • Screen specsPurpose, layout, every numbered state, accessibility, decisions with reasons, and the conflicts that block sign-off.
  • Design tokensOne canonical stylesheet, plus a generated export in the W3C standard format.
  • Component previewsReal HTML on real tokens, every state marked. No build step, no network.

Figma · Penpot · Sketch · paper

The designer's tool

  • The imported token setTokens Studio, Penpot tokens, Style Dictionary, or any tool that reads the standard.
  • Wireframes and hi-fi framesOne frame per numbered state. The numbering is the checklist. Frames are not checked in.
  • Exploration and critiqueVariants, comment threads, and annotation, where designers already work well.

One way only

Nothing is authored downstream and pulled back. That would put a Gate 1 artifact outside version control. The token export is generated from the stylesheet, and CI fails if the two disagree. So the palette that a designer draws with cannot drift from the palette that ships. A design-tool-first workflow cannot give that guarantee. That is the whole argument for the direction of the arrow.

Why /ux became its own persona

Design used to be a sketch pasted into a story, and one persona covered both crafts. When design became CI-enforced, that persona produced artifacts that no one with design authority reviewed. When you enforce an artifact, you find out whether its role has an owner.

The honest limitation

This does not capture the collaboration features of a design tool: comment threads, handoff annotation, visual history. The collaboration that a gate depends on (which states exist, which conflicts are open, who owns them) is now in reviewable files. The rest stays in the tool.

07Tracking

A board for everyone without repository access

Clients, some managers, and testers need to follow this work, and they will never open a pull request. Jira is the board that they already read. It carries delivery tracking, a client-readable view, and test evidence. It carries no approval.

Approval never travels, in either direction.

A ticket moved to Done, a sign-off field, a comment that says "approved": none of these opens or closes a gate. A pull-request review authenticates who approved exactly which bytes. A ticket field authenticates nothing after it crosses an integration boundary, and what was approved is no longer recoverable. That is the one thing this framework will not forward through an integration.

Who does what with it

/ba, on request

Creates the epic, story, and change-request tickets when the human asks. A story that is not yet approved says Draft — awaiting approval. So the board never implies agreement that does not exist.

/qa, bugs and test evidence

One test ticket per acceptance criterion. Its result comes from a real pipeline run. A criterion with no automated test reads Not yet automated. Nobody gets a pass that nobody earned.

/manager, keeps it current

Mirrors delivery status from GitHub and cross-links tickets to their specifications. Outward reporting is inside its charter. It still holds no gate authority in either system.

/architect, never writes

Its read-only guarantee is a tool restriction on files. That restriction does not reach an external system. So this one is a charter rule, not tool-enforced. The framework states this plainly instead of pretending.

Five templates, filled from the specification

TicketBuilt fromCarries
EpicAn epic artifactGoal, its stories, open decisions
StoryA user storyWhat it delivers, the requirements behind it, every acceptance criterion, its screens, what is still undecided
TestOne acceptance criterionWhat is proven, the result, and the pipeline run it came from
BugA defect issueWhat goes wrong, what must happen, how to see it, which criterion it breaks
Change requestA change issueThe ask in the requester's words, what it affects, who decides

Each ticket deep-links to its specification. So nobody reads the spec on the board. They see the state of the spec there. Sprints, assignees, and estimates stay the business of the tracker. If we mirror those into the repository, we recreate the status-file habit that this framework was rebuilt to remove.

Kept honest by construction

One writer, dry run by default

Every write goes through one tool. By default the tool prints the exact text that a client will read and changes nothing. So a pull request reviews client-facing wording before it reaches a live board. That review needs no credentials.

A refusal list in code

The tool refuses status transitions, sign-off fields, sprint, assignee, estimate, and worklog, whatever a template or an instruction asks for. Check 12 rejects a template that even declares one.

Losing the tracker cannot break the build

A recorded ticket number is a convenience for humans, not a governance edge. A missing number is a warning. That is the test of whether an integration kept to its job.

09Where we are

Framework state, and what needs a decision

This repository is the home of the framework, not a product. It was extracted from the logistics reference project on 3 August 2026 and is at version 0.3.0. Its traceability manifest is empty on purpose. The seven decision records are the only tracked IDs here.

Shipped since extraction Green

A deterministic scaffolder, so a team installs with npx github:ss-trigent/aidlc and updates with --update · task classification into Simple, Medium, and Complex tiers, with a project-owned task-surfaces.md to extend it · the framework hash lock (check 14) and a version stamp · Windows and CRLF-tolerant tools · the opt-in browser-test layer with a reviewed plan per story and cross-repo evidence (ADR-006, check 15) · development spec packages and the two-gate development cycle (ADR-007, check 16) · the DEV persona no longer commits or pushes · sixteen validator checks with their own test suite in tools/*.test.mjs, run by CI.

Recorded, awaiting review Proposed

ADR-005, the Cursor, opencode, and Copilot persona surfaces (3 August) · ADR-006, the browser-test layer (18 August). Both are in use. Neither has an architect verdict yet.

Not built Gaps

No code, deployment, or monitoring edge in the graph · the Gate 3 deploy tail and its operations backlog activate at the first real environment · Claude Design sync is recorded but not enabled · security, accessibility, and performance checks are human-review items, not automated scans · a persona can still, in theory, merge or close, because no tool enforces that limit.

Decisions requested from the architect

The governing decision records Accepted

ADR-001 (three gates, seven personas, single vendor), ADR-002 (Jira for tracking, never approval), ADR-003 (requirements, then design, then stories), and ADR-004 (two-phase design, the architecture deliverable, advisory planning) are accepted and merged on main. ADR-007 (spec packages and the two-gate development cycle) is accepted, 19 August.

Branch protection Blocking everything

Make the repository public, or upgrade the plan. Until then the enforcement spine runs on convention. This is the highest-value item available, and it is a setting, not code.

Multi-tool surfaces ADR-005 proposed

You can type the personas in Cursor, opencode, and Copilot. They are generated from the Claude sources, and drift fails CI. The decision record that scopes the Anthropic-only position of ADR-001 awaits review. The read-only guarantee is tool-enforced only where the tool allows it (Claude Code, opencode). Elsewhere it is a stated charter rule.

Browser-test layer ADR-006 proposed

Playwright as an opt-in layer, with two storage options: tests in the product repo, which can block a story PR, or tests in a QA repo, which publish an evidence file that CI validates but cannot make blocking. Playwright MCP is the one write-capable MCP tool, scoped to a test environment. The trade-offs await review.

Does the graph need a code edge? Open
